Chase, Visa and Mastercard vendors leaked customer emails. Expect bank emails that know your name, and teach your team where to check instead

The three companies say no card numbers or payment data were taken, but a leak of names, email addresses and some phone numbers is exactly what scammers need to send convincing fake bank messages. Here's what to tell anyone in your office who handles a company card.

JPMorgan Chase, Visa and Mastercard have each confirmed incidents at outside service providers after an extortion group called N0n posted data it says came from them, per SecurityWeek. Chase said on X that the attackers accessed "certain marketing data held by those vendors, which may include some customer names and email addresses," and that its services were not disrupted, per The Register.

What was taken, and what wasn't. Visa said the incident involved a third-party provider in Asia Pacific and that no payment data such as card numbers was affected. Mastercard said a limited number of email addresses from cardholders in one benefit program were exposed, and its payment network was not affected, per The Register. Reuters' review of the Chase-related files found over 16,000 customer emails, some with phone numbers and postal codes, per SecurityWeek. None of the three has named the vendors or said how many customers were affected.

Why it still matters to a small business. No card numbers doesn't mean no risk. A real name, a real email, a phone number and the fact that you bank with Chase or carry a certain card is what makes a fake "fraud alert" believable. If your office uses a Chase account or a Visa or Mastercard company card, assume the people who handle it may get those messages soon.

Watch for fake "credit monitoring" offers too. Chase said it plans to reach out to affected customers where appropriate and offer free credit monitoring, per SecurityWeek. Scammers copy offers like that. Don't sign up through a link in an email or text. Log in to your account the usual way and look for the offer there.

Action items for today.
1. Tell whoever handles the company card or bank account. One short message: emails, texts or calls about this breach may be fake, even if they use your name.
2. Check through the app or the number on the card. Never through a link, a reply or a phone number in the message. Type the bank's address yourself or use its app.
3. Never read a one-time code to anyone. A caller who says they're from the bank's fraud team and asks for the code you just got is the scam.
4. Slow down payment changes. Any request to move money, add a payee or change account details gets confirmed by phone, using a number you already had.
5. Know which vendors hold your customer list. This leak came from marketing vendors, not the banks themselves. Make a short list of the email, marketing and loyalty tools that hold your customers' names and emails, and make sure each one has MFA on and only the people who need it have access.
